\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related_work}
Animal re-identification (Re-ID) aims to recognize individual animals across re-encounters using visual appearance.
In contrast to many human re-ID deployments, wildlife monitoring typically operates in \textbf{open-set} conditions (new individuals continually appear), with \textbf{limited labeled data}, strong \textbf{pose/background variation}, and frequent \textbf{occlusions} (e.g., vegetation and partial camera-trap captures). A comprehensive review of the early progression from hand-crafted pipelines to deep learning approaches is provided by Schneider et al.~\cite{re-id-overview}. This chapter focuses on (i) \textbf{species-agnostic} methods that can transfer across taxa, (ii) \textbf{hybrid and fusion} pipelines that combine global and local evidence, and (iii) work connecting re-ID to \textbf{population size estimation} with humans in the loop.

\section{Local Feature Matching and Classical Pipelines}
Early animal re-ID systems relied on hand-crafted local descriptors and explicit matching.
HotSpotter~\cite{6475023} is a representative example: it detects keypoints, describes them with SIFT~\cite{Lowe2004SIFT}, and identifies individuals by aggregating evidence from distinctive local matches (``hot spots'').
Because these pipelines do not require training on identity labels, they are inherently \textbf{species-agnostic} and remain attractive when labeled data is scarce.
Recent work has also revisited the strong baseline of ``match-count'' style similarity: \cite{jmse11050889} compares SIFT/SURF to learned local features (SuperPoint~\cite{DeTone2018SuperPoint}) and shows that the number of reliable keypoint correspondences can be a powerful signal for individual identity.

The limitation of purely hand-crafted pipelines is that their matching heuristic is fixed: they often under-utilize global context and can be brittle under extreme viewpoint changes, scale differences, motion blur, or background clutter. Nevertheless, the underlying principle of \textbf{geometric consistency} remains central in modern systems: learned local descriptors (e.g., DISK~\cite{tyszkiewicz2020disklearninglocalfeatures} or SuperPoint~\cite{DeTone2018SuperPoint}) and learned matchers (e.g., LightGlue~\cite{lindenberger2023lightgluelocalfeaturematching}) can be integrated into the same geometric verification framework that classical pipelines used.

\section{Species-Specific Trait-Based Re-Identification}
Another line of research targets species-specific biometric traits (e.g., elephant ears, shark fins, whale flukes).
Instead of generic features, these methods isolate a task-relevant structure and perform shape- or template-based matching.
For example, CurvRank~\cite{ani12151980} and finFindR~\cite{finFindR} match fin/fluke contours to identify individuals.
Trait-based approaches often require \textbf{less identity-labeled data} (a detector/segmenter for the trait can be trained independently), and they can achieve high performance when the trait is consistently visible.
However, they are typically \textbf{not transferable}: each pipeline must be redesigned for a new species and imaging setup. This lack of generality is a major barrier in practice, where conservation projects often span multiple species and camera-trap deployments.

\section{Deep Embedding Models and Species-Agnostic Benchmarks}
Deep learning approaches replace explicit matching heuristics with learned representations.
Most modern systems map an image to an embedding vector such that images of the same individual are nearby, while different individuals are far apart.
Training can be done either as closed-set classification (over a fixed set of identities) or via metric learning; the latter is generally preferred for wildlife monitoring because it naturally supports open-set retrieval.

Recent progress has been accelerated by large-scale multi-dataset training and standardized benchmarking.
The WildlifeDatasets toolkit~\cite{čermák2023wildlifedatasetsopensourcetoolkitanimal} provides unified access to diverse animal re-ID datasets and splits, enabling reproducible evaluation across species.
Within this ecosystem, MegaDescriptor-style models provide strong \textbf{species-agnostic} global embeddings that can be used in a zero-shot retrieval setting.
At the same time, large-scale training introduces an evaluation pitfall that is often under-discussed in the literature: if training data overlaps with evaluation benchmarks (or if near-duplicates leak across splits), reported accuracy can be overly optimistic. This thesis explicitly highlights split consistency and leakage risks when comparing methods across datasets and baselines.

\section{Hybrid Local-Global Representations and Fusion}
Hybrid approaches attempt to combine the robustness of global embeddings with the discriminative detail of local evidence.
Nepovinnykh et al.~\cite{springerSpeciesAgnosticPatterned} propose a species-agnostic patterned re-ID pipeline that aggregates deep local features to form a more invariant representation, bridging classical local matching and modern deep embeddings.

A closely related line of work treats re-ID as a \textbf{score fusion} problem.
WildFusion~\cite{cermak2024wildfusionindividualanimalidentification} fuses multiple similarity scores (e.g., global deep similarity and local matching similarity) using explicit \textbf{calibration} to make scores comparable across matchers and datasets. Their results show that calibrated fusion can substantially improve mean accuracy across diverse benchmarks. The trade-off is computational cost: combining multiple heavy matchers and calibration steps can be expensive, which motivates research into cheaper pipelines that still preserve complementary evidence.

\section{Robustness to Background Bias and Part Alignment}
A recurring real-world failure mode is that models exploit spurious correlations: background, lighting, and camera placement may correlate with identity labels in a dataset, but do not generalize.
Yu et al.~\cite{yu2024elephant} explicitly frame this as the ``elephant in the room'': they demonstrate that robust animal re-ID requires aligning informative animal parts and reducing reliance on background context. Their approach performs \textbf{unsupervised part-based feature alignment}, improving robustness under pose changes and clutter without requiring dense part annotations.
This perspective is also consistent with practical preprocessing choices used in many pipelines, such as detector-based cropping or segmentation, which aim to increase invariance by focusing the model on the animal itself.

\section{Human-in-the-Loop Re-ID and Population Estimation}
Individual identification is often a means to an end: population monitoring and conservation decisions.
Perez et al.~\cite{perez2024humanintheloopvisualreidpopulation} propose \emph{Human-in-the-Loop Nested Importance Sampling} (HITL-NIS), a statistically grounded estimator that queries an oracle (a human annotator) for a small number of same/different pair judgments to estimate the number of individuals without fully clustering the dataset.
This framing makes explicit that annotation is a limited resource and that uncertainty should be propagated into the estimate.

However, in field deployments, oracle decisions are not perfect.
Johansson et al.~\cite{johansson2020identificationerrors} show that identification errors in camera-trap workflows can systematically bias population estimates, emphasizing the need to account for human mistakes rather than assuming error-free supervision.
This thesis directly builds on this gap by evaluating HITL-NIS under \textbf{simulated oracle label noise} to quantify robustness as a function of error rate and confirmation strictness.

\subsection{Contributions of this thesis}
Positioned within this landscape, this thesis contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{unified, species-agnostic} pipeline that connects individual re-identification to downstream \textbf{population size estimation} within a single project and evaluation protocol.
    \item A \textbf{hybrid local-global design} that fuses global embeddings with Fisher-vector local evidence and optional geometric verification, aiming to approach strong fusion baselines while reducing runtime.
    \item \textbf{Transparent runtime reporting} and discussion of practical trade-offs, highlighting that high-performing fusion methods (e.g., WildFusion) can be substantially more expensive to run end-to-end.
    \item An explicit emphasis on \textbf{evaluation hygiene}, including discussion of split consistency and the risk of underreported \textbf{data leakage} when training data overlaps with benchmark evaluations.
    \item An extension of HITL-NIS evaluation to account for \textbf{human labeling errors}, quantifying how population estimates degrade as oracle reliability decreases and motivating confirmation-based strategies.
\end{itemize}
